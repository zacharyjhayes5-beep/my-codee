There is no version of this where adding a teenage driver is cheap. But there is
a meaningful difference between families who plan for it and families who find
out at renewal.

## Call before they get the license, not after

Two reasons. First, most policies require you to list a licensed household
driver, and gaps here cause claim problems. Second, this is your chance to
restructure — which car they are assigned to matters more than almost anything
else.

## Assign them to the right car

Rates follow the driver *and* the vehicle. A new driver on the newest car with
the lowest deductible is the most expensive combination available. Assigning
them to an older, safe, lower-value vehicle is usually the single biggest lever
you have.

Safe does not mean slow and old for its own sake — modern safety features matter
and often earn their own discount. It means avoiding the high-horsepower and
high-repair-cost vehicles.

## Ask for these discounts by name

- **Good student.** Typically requires a B average or better. Bring the report card.
- **Driver training.** Completion of an approved course.
- **Student away at school.** If they are at college more than a set distance
  away without a car, the rate can drop substantially.
- **Telematics.** A usage-based program that measures actual driving. For a
  careful teen this can be worth real money — and it gives you visibility.

## Revisit your liability limits at the same time

This is the uncomfortable part, and it matters. A new driver raises the odds of
an at-fault accident, and liability is what protects your house and savings if
one is serious. Many families are underinsured exactly when their exposure goes
up.

Raising limits costs far less than most people assume — often less than the
increase from adding the driver in the first place. An umbrella policy is worth
pricing too.

## Then keep it updated

Tell me when they go to college, when they come back, and when they buy their
own car. Each of those is a rating change, and they do not happen automatically.
