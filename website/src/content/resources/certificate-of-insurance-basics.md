If you run a trade or contract business, you have had this conversation: the job
is ready, and someone in an office needs "a COI" before you can set foot on the
site. Here is what they are actually asking for.

## What a certificate of insurance is

A COI is a one-page proof of coverage. It lists your policies, your limits, and
your effective dates. It is *evidence*, not coverage itself — it does not change
your policy or extend it to anyone.

Your agent issues it, usually same day. If someone needs one, send them to me
with the exact wording they want and I will handle it.

## "Additional insured" is the part that matters

Most contracts do not just want proof — they want to be named as an **additional
insured** on your general liability policy. That means your policy extends to
cover them for claims arising out of *your* work.

This is a real change to your policy, made by endorsement, and it is why the
request cannot always be answered in thirty seconds. Some contracts also ask for
a **waiver of subrogation** or **primary and non-contributory** wording, which
are separate endorsements again.

## Read the insurance section before you sign

The insurance requirements in a contract are usually one boring page in the
middle. Read it, and send it over before you sign, because it will tell you:

- The limits you must carry — sometimes higher than what you have
- Whether umbrella coverage is required
- Whether workers compensation is required, even if you have no employees
- The exact entity names to list, which have to match precisely

Finding out you need a $2 million limit on the morning of the start date is an
avoidable problem.

## Keep it current

Certificates carry expiration dates that mirror your policy term. Larger general
contractors track those dates and will lock you out of the site when they lapse.
When your policy renews, tell me who needs an updated copy and I will send them
out.
